What Counts as a Dental Emergency?

Not every dental issue is an emergency, but many are more urgent than people think. You should seek emergency dental care if you have severe or lasting pain, bleeding that will not stop, swelling in the gums or face, a loose or knocked-out adult tooth, or a tooth that has broken and exposed sensitive layers. Signs of infection, such as a bad taste, a bump on the gum, fever or swelling that moves toward the eye or neck, should never be ignored.

Smaller problems, such as a tiny chip with no pain or a lost filling without sensitivity, can sometimes wait a day or two. Even so, a short phone call helps you decide whether you need an urgent dental appointment today or a regular visit later in the week. When in doubt, call. Pain is your body's alarm, and early treatment is almost always simpler and less expensive.

    • A toothache can come from decay, a cracked tooth, gum disease, a damaged filling or an infection.   • Dental Abscess Treatment

    • A dental abscess is a pocket of pus caused by bacteria. It can form at the root of a tooth or in the gum beside it. Symptoms include throbbing pain, heat and cold sensitivity, swelling, a bad taste and sometimes fever. An abscess does not heal by itself and can become dangerous if it spreads. Prompt dental abscess treatment may include draining the infection, medication when appropriate, and treating the cause with a root canal or extraction. If swelling affects your breathing or swallowing, go to the nearest hospital emergency room right away.

  • Emergency Root Canal

    • Root canals have an unfair reputation. The goal is to end pain, not cause it. When the tissue inside a tooth becomes infected or inflamed, an emergency root canal removes the damaged tissue, cleans the inside of the tooth and seals it. The tooth is then restored with a filling or crown. With modern anesthesia, most patients describe the experience as similar to getting a deep filling. Saving your natural tooth is usually better for chewing, appearance and the health of nearby teeth.

  • Emergency Tooth Extraction

    • We always try to save a tooth first, but sometimes removal is the safest choice. A tooth may be too fractured, too decayed or too damaged by infection to repair. In those cases, an emergency tooth extraction removes the source of pain and infection. We numb the area thoroughly, explain each step and give clear aftercare instructions. Afterward, we can discuss options such as a bridge or an implant so the gap does not cause bite problems later.

  • Broken Tooth Repair

    • Teeth can break from biting something hard, from a fall, from an old filling failing or from grinding at night. Broken tooth repair depends on how deep the damage goes. A small break may need only bonding, a larger one may need a crown, and a fracture that reaches the nerve may require a root canal first. If you can find the broken piece, keep it in milk or saliva and bring it with you. Rinse gently, avoid chewing on that side and call us immediately.

  • Chipped Tooth Fix

    • Chips are among the most common dental injuries, and many are easy to correct. A chipped tooth fix may involve smoothing a rough edge, tooth-colored bonding or a veneer for a highly visible tooth. Even a small chip can leave a sharp edge that cuts the tongue or cheek, and it can weaken the tooth over time. Having it checked quickly protects your comfort and your smile.

  • Dental Trauma Repair

    • Falls, sports injuries, car accidents and fights can damage teeth, gums and the jaw. Dental trauma repair may include re-implanting a knocked-out tooth, splinting a loose tooth, stitching torn gum tissue and repairing fractures. Time is critical for a knocked-out adult tooth. Pick it up by the crown, never the root. If it is dirty, rinse it briefly with water without scrubbing. Try to place it back in the socket, or store it in a small container of milk, and see a dentist within an hour if possible. After any head injury, watch for dizziness, vomiting or confusion and seek medical care if they appear.

What to Do Before You Reach the Dentist

What you do in the first hour can change the outcome. Here is a simple checklist for emergency oral care at home:

  • Rinse gently with warm water or a mild salt water solution to clean the area.
  • Control swelling with a cold compress against your cheek for 15 to 20 minutes at a time.
  • Manage pain safely using an over-the-counter pain reliever according to the label and your doctor's guidance.
  • Save any pieces by keeping broken fragments or a knocked-out tooth in milk.
  • Control bleeding by pressing clean gauze against the area for several minutes.
  • Avoid hard and sticky foods and chew on the opposite side.
  • Skip heat on swelling, because heat can encourage an infection to spread.
  • Call right away at 773-373-0971 so we can prepare for your arrival.

Protecting Children and Athletes From Dental Injuries

Children and active adults face a higher risk of dental trauma. A properly fitted mouthguard is one of the most effective protections for anyone who plays contact sports, rides a bike or skateboards. If a child's baby tooth is knocked out, do not try to put it back in, but do call a dentist to check the area. If a permanent tooth is knocked out, treat it as a true emergency and seek care immediately. Teach children to tell an adult as soon as something hurts, because early reporting leads to better results.

Preventing the Next Dental Emergency

Not every emergency can be avoided, but many can. Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, floss daily and schedule professional cleanings and exams at least twice a year. Do not use your teeth to open packages, bite fingernails or chew ice. If you grind your teeth at night, ask about a night guard. Replace worn fillings and treat cavities while they are small. A few minutes of prevention each day can save you from pain, lost time and unexpected expense.
